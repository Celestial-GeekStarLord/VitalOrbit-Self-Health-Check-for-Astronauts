Why these checks matter

NASA's Human Research Program groups the dangers of human spaceflight into five hazards. NASA notes that these hazards do not stand alone: they can feed off one another and worsen the effects on the body. VitalOrbit tracks signals that show these effects early.

Space radiation

What NASA says
Outside Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere, galactic cosmic rays and solar storms (solar particle events and coronal mass ejections) can harm the body and the spacecraft. Astronauts cannot see or feel radiation. Exposure raises cancer risk, can damage the central nervous system, alter cognitive function, reduce motor function and prompt behavioral changes.
What CSA adds
Neutron radiation makes up about 30% of total radiation on the ISS and can cause serious biological damage. CSA is funding the Canadian Active Neutron Spectrometer (CANS), a compact instrument that measures neutron exposure continuously and autonomously.
NASA limits
NASA's standard (NASA-STD-3001) sets a career limit of under 600 mSv, the same for all ages and sexes, and 250 mSv for a single solar particle event.
What VitalOrbit tracks
Your dosimeter dose against the 600 mSv limit and live NASA space-weather alerts on the Radiation page, plus mood, sleep and temperature.

NASA: Space radiation

Isolation and confinement

What NASA says
Behavioral responses occur among groups of people far from Earth who are isolated and confined in a small space for a long time. Crews must be carefully chosen, trained and supported to work as a team for months or years.
What VitalOrbit tracks
Mood and sleep hours, plus a daily social-contact mission.
Source
NASA: Isolation and confinement

Distance from Earth

What NASA says
Instructions, new supplies and medical care become harder to get as astronauts go deeper into space. Mars is on average 140 million miles away. Instead of a three-day lunar trip, astronauts would be away for roughly three years, so planning and self-sufficiency are key.
What VitalOrbit tracks
Everything, stored on the device so it works without a link, with an option to send your log to the ground when contact is available.
Source
NASA: Distance from Earth

Gravity fields

What NASA says
Muscles, bones, inner ear and organs must adjust to the gravity on the station or spacecraft, on the Moon or Mars, and again on Earth after returning home. On Mars astronauts would live in three-eighths of Earth's gravity for up to two years.
What VitalOrbit tracks
Heart rate, blood pressure and daily exercise minutes.
Source
NASA: Gravity fields

Hostile and closed environments

What NASA says
A spacecraft is both a home and a machine. Keeping it safe means managing temperature, pressure, lighting and noise, monitoring microbial communities and tracking immune responses.
What VitalOrbit tracks
Body temperature, blood glucose and sleep, plus a daily fluids mission.
Source
NASA: Hostile and closed environments

Where the reference ranges come from

The hazard descriptions above come from NASA and CSA. The green, amber and red ranges for each reading do not: NASA's public hazard pages do not publish astronaut vital-sign limits. VitalOrbit uses common adult clinical reference ranges, simplified for a quick check (for example, fasting glucose of 70 to 99 mg/dL as normal and 126 mg/dL or more as high, and resting blood pressure near 120/80 mmHg). A flight surgeon sets limits for each crew member, so treat the colors as a prompt to look closer, not a diagnosis.

Contact the flight surgeon when

  • Any indicator shows "Act now" (red) and a re-measurement after rest gives the same result.
  • Several indicators show "Watch" (amber) at once, or one keeps drifting the same way for several days.
  • You have chest pain, fainting, severe headache, confusion or trouble breathing. Treat this as an emergency and follow your crew emergency procedures.
  • Your mood stays low, you have thoughts of harming yourself, or you cannot sleep for several nights. Ask for support right away.
